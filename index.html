Build 01 · biomedical · AI · 3D

heart3D

An ECG is twelve flat shadows of one arrow swinging through the heart. heart3D rebuilds that arrow in 3D from real hospital recordings, plays it on a real heart shape with the electrical wave sweeping across it, and lets two AI models read the trace and show which part of the heartbeat drove their answer.

  • 21,799 real ECGs
  • 0.93 AUC, 5 diagnosis groups
  • 19 CT scans behind the heart

A gradient-boosted model on measurements and a 1D ResNet on raw signals, both close to published benchmarks. Live mode streams an ECG in as if from a bedside monitor. A learning tool, not a medical device.

Python · NumPy · scikit-learn · PyTorch · signal processing · three.js

Build 03 · quant research · honesty

The gold bot

I put 33 strategies through one honest simulator on 20 years of gold prices, with real spreads, financing costs, a luck correction, and the last two years sealed until the very end. Almost nothing survived. Every 1-minute scalp lost about 100% after costs. The one rule that passed its sealed exam then failed on seven other markets, so it only runs on a demo account, with a kill switch and 23 tests.

Python · pandas · NumPy · MetaTrader 5 API · Dukascopy & FRED data · unittest

Build 06 · the one that didn't work

The idea I killed

A while ago I got hooked on an idea: two Bendix-style dual magnetos, a specific gear ratio and a few batteries, set up so the system would keep making more energy than it used.

The more I worked it out, the clearer it got. It was a perpetual-motion machine. A magneto turns the rotation you give it into electricity and always gives back a little less. No gear ratio changes that. So I stopped.

I didn't stop thinking about it, though. Magnetos are tough little generators that don't need a battery to work. Pointed a different way, at rotation that's already happening and going to waste, they could still be useful. That version is still on my drawing board.

An essay · with switches

Are generational curses just genetics?

There was a season when I questioned everything that was happening to me. I started wondering if some of it came from further back than me, the kind of thing people call a generational curse. So I went looking.

I found three things that really do pass down a family. Switch them on one at a time and watch where they go.

Every switch is off. All you can see is a family.

Genes. You get a random half of each parent's DNA. A variant can raise the odds of something, like anxiety or addiction, but it's a coin flip at every generation, and odds aren't fate.

Epigenetics. Experience can flip chemical switches on genes (like DNA methylation) without changing the DNA itself. Most are wiped clean between generations. People conceived during the Dutch Hunger Winter still carried marks of it decades later. In mice, some marks seem to reach the grandchildren. In humans, whether they go that far is still debated.

Family patterns. How a family handles conflict, money, fear and love gets learned, not inherited. Of the three, this passes down most reliably, and it's also the one you can most change.

All three on. Together they explained more than I expected. They didn't explain everything.

I didn't get a conclusion I could fully sit with. I learned something real about epigenetics, and I came out of a hard season understanding myself better. But I've left a little room for whatever science can't measure yet.

Here's what I learned, and where I stopped.

Further reading: Heijmans et al. (2008) on the Dutch Hunger Winter · Dias & Ressler (2014) on inherited fear in mice · and plenty of arguments about both.
